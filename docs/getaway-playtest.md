# Getaway group playtest

Start with four people and one projector, then repeat with ten. Include at least one person who rarely plays action games. Use the finished local build and real phones on the same Wi-Fi.

1. Let the game teach its controls. Do not explain the strategy or assign escorts before the first match.
2. Play one complete match. Watch whether players choose different haul sizes, help a loaded teammate, use both routes, and regain control after being shoved.
3. Ask each person what they were trying to do and what felt unfair or unclear. Ask the least experienced player first so the loudest player does not establish the answer.
4. Play again. Observe whether players change their plan and whether a defended delivery remains possible.

The key evidence is a player giving up their own collection run to help a teammate get home. Also note whether five bags becomes automatic, empty-handed camping dominates, or the final thirty seconds becomes dead time.

At ten players, check identity and threat readability from the back of the room, the balance between effects and conversation, and any stuck-input or stuttering reports. Lower volume if it masks speech; record the setting.

A successful technical run cannot answer these questions. Tune the existing slowdown, supply, approach geometry and recovery windows before adding new mechanics.

## Odd crews

Repeat with nine players (4 vs 5), seven (3 vs 4), then five (2 vs 3). The smaller crew earns a scoring bonus of ×1.25, ×1.33 (exactly 4/3 internally), or ×1.5 respectively. Compare adjusted points, not raw bag totals.

Swap people between the larger and smaller crews across matches. Observe whether the extra interceptor outweighs the smaller crew's bonus, whether both sides can escort a carrier, and whether anyone feels their contribution is futile. The two-versus-three match is the hardest balance test. A scoring bonus does not prove those matches are fair; record the difference before considering any further handicap.
